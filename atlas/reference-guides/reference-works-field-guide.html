A Field Guide to Reference Works

Some books are meant to be read; others are meant to be consulted. This is a working guide to the second kind — thirty-four families of reference work, each defined, sorted by the question it answers, and matched to a real example you could pull off a shelf today.

Section I

The Consultable Book

A reference work is a book you do not read from beginning to end. You open it at a point of need, extract exactly what you came for, and close it again. Its unit is not the chapter but the entry; its virtue is not narrative but retrieval.

That single design decision — organise for lookup, not for reading — is what separates a dictionary from a novel, a gazetteer from a travelogue, an almanac from an essay. Everything else about reference works follows from it: the alphabetical ordering, the dense abbreviations, the indexes and cross-references, the columns of small type. A reference book is a machine for answering questions, and its parts are engineered so the answer surfaces quickly.

The impulse is very old. The Roman scholar Pliny the Elder assembled his Naturalis Historia (c. 77 CE) as a browsable compendium of some twenty thousand facts, complete with a summary table so a reader could jump straight to a topic — arguably the ancestor of the modern encyclopedia. The tenth-century Byzantine Suda was a vast alphabetical lexicon-cum-encyclopedia of the ancient world. Muslim scholars built enormous biographical dictionaries (ṭabaqāt) and hadith indexes to keep a growing tradition retrievable. Every literate civilisation, once its stock of knowledge outgrew a single memory, invented tools to file it.

The question a reference work answers is never “what is the story?” but “where, exactly, is the fact I need?”

What follows sorts that toolkit into thirty-four families. The distinctions are real but not always sharp — a handbook shades into a manual, a lexicon into a dictionary, a digest into an index — and many real books wear two hats at once. Treat the categories as a map of a territory rather than a set of sealed boxes. The searchable field guide in Section III lets you filter the whole set by the field it serves and the question it answers.

Section II

The Questions They Answer

The fastest way to choose the right reference work is to start not from the book but from the question. Each family of reference is, in effect, a specialised answer to one kind of query. Match the question to the tool and the search half-solves itself.

“What does this word mean?”

→Dictionary

“What is this thing?”

→Encyclopedia

“Where is it?”

→Atlas or Gazetteer

“When did it happen?”

→Chronology

“Who was this person?”

→Biographical dictionary

“How do I do it?”

→Handbook or Manual

“What are the current facts?”

→Almanac or Statistical Abstract

“Where can I learn more?”

→Bibliography

“Where does this word occur?”

→Concordance

“How is this field organised?”

→Companion or Sourcebook

A note on overlap Notice that several questions have two good answers. “Where is it?” splits between an atlas (which shows you the place) and a gazetteer (which describes it in words). “How do I do it?” splits between a handbook (which gathers the facts and formulas you apply yourself) and a manual (which walks you through a procedure step by step). These twins recur throughout the field guide below; where the boundary is genuinely blurry, the guide says so.

01Language

Dictionary

Defines words — their meanings, spelling, pronunciation, part of speech, and history.

SeeThe Oxford English Dictionary — a historical dictionary that traces each of its 600,000+ words through millions of dated quotations.
02General

Encyclopedia

Explains topics in survey articles — history, science, the arts, biography, ideas.

SeeEncyclopædia Britannica — founded in Edinburgh in 1768; it ended its 244-year print run in 2012 and continues online.
03General

Almanac

Gathers annual facts and figures — weather, tides, astronomy, records, government data.

SeeThe World Almanac and Book of Facts — published annually since 1868 and long the best-selling U.S. book of facts.
04General

Atlas

Shows where things are — political, physical, historical, and thematic maps.

SeeThe Times Comprehensive Atlas of the World — the benchmark English-language world atlas, first issued in 1922.
05General

Gazetteer

Describes places in words — towns, rivers, mountains — with population, location, and detail.

SeeThe Columbia Gazetteer of the World — roughly 170,000 entries describing the world's places.
06Science

Handbook

Packs a field's working facts, constants, and formulas into one dense practical volume.

SeeThe CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics — the “Rubber Bible,” published yearly since 1914.
07General

Manual

Walks you through a procedure — how to operate, assemble, maintain, or repair something.

SeeHaynes Manuals — car-repair guides written from a full teardown and rebuild of each model.
08General

Companion

Maps a whole subject in alphabetical background entries — authors, works, terms, movements.

SeeThe Oxford Companion to English Literature — the standard one-volume guide to its field.
09General

Guidebook

Offers practical advice for doing something on the ground — usually travel, place by place.

SeeThe Michelin Guide — practical travel and dining guidance whose stars still make and break restaurants.
10Science

Field Guide

Helps you identify what you're looking at in the wild — birds, plants, insects, minerals.

SeeA Field Guide to the Birds by Roger Tory Peterson (1934) — its arrows to key field marks founded modern bird ID.
11Science

Catalogue

A systematic, numbered listing of items in a collection — stars, artworks, specimens, products.

SeeThe Messier Catalogue — Charles Messier's list of 110 deep-sky objects (M1–M110), compiled in the 1770s–80s.
12Business

Directory

Lists who and where — businesses, organisations, or professionals with contact details.

SeeThe Thomas Register of American Manufacturers — the classic “who makes what” industrial directory.
13Business

Yearbook

An annual snapshot — the year's events, statistics, and standings, refreshed each edition.

SeeThe Statesman's Yearbook — country-by-country facts and figures, published every year since 1864.
14History

Chronology

Arranges events in time — a timeline that fixes what happened, and in what order.

SeeThe Timetables of History (Bernard Grun) — parallel columns showing what happened at once across politics, science, and art.
15Religion

Concordance

Lists every occurrence of every word in a text, keyed to its exact location.

SeeStrong's Exhaustive Concordance (1890) for the Bible; المعجم المفهرس لألفاظ القرآن الكريم, ʿAbd al-Bāqī's Qurʾānic concordance (1945).
16General

Index

A finding aid — points you from a topic to where it is discussed, within a book or across many.

SeeThe Readers' Guide to Periodical Literature — the classic subject index to popular magazine articles.
17General

Bibliography

A curated list of the sources on a subject — where to go to read further.

SeeThe MLA International Bibliography — the standard record of scholarship in language and literature.
18Science

Abstract Index

Summarises the research literature so you can scan a field and find the papers that matter.

SeeChemical Abstracts — summaries of the world's chemistry literature since 1907, now the CAS databases.
19Language

Thesaurus

Groups words by meaning — synonyms and antonyms — to help you find the word you want.

SeeRoget's Thesaurus — Peter Mark Roget's 1852 classification of words by idea rather than by alphabet.
20Language

Lexicon

A dictionary for a specialised language or corpus — classical Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, the law.

SeeLane's Arabic–English Lexicon (8 vols, 1863–93), drawn from the medieval Arabic dictionaries; Liddell & Scott for Greek.
21Language

Glossary

A short list of terms defined for one subject or one book — its own private dictionary.

SeeThe term list appended to a technical or legal textbook, defining the vocabulary that book uses.
22Language

Corpus

A searchable body of real texts used to study how a language is actually used.

SeeThe Corpus of Contemporary American English (COCA) — about one billion words (1990–2019), built by Mark Davies.
23Language

Corpus Dictionary

A dictionary written from a corpus, defining words by real usage and frequency rather than intuition.

SeeThe Collins COBUILD Dictionary (1987) — the first dictionary built entirely on corpus evidence.
24Business

Statistical Abstract

Assembles a nation's key numbers — population, economy, society — into one browsable data book.

SeeThe Statistical Abstract of the United States — issued by the Census Bureau 1878–2012, and by ProQuest since 2013.
25Science

Formula Book

A compact store of equations and identities, so you look up a formula instead of deriving it.

SeeCRC Standard Mathematical Tables and Formulae — the standing desk-reference of equations.
26Science

Tables

Pre-computed numerical values for lookup — logarithms, function values, steam properties.

SeeAbramowitz & Stegun's Handbook of Mathematical Functions (1964) — the famous tables of function values.
27Law

Code Book

The binding rules of a trade or jurisdiction — standards you must build, wire, or operate by.

SeeThe National Electrical Code (NFPA 70) — the adopted U.S. standard for safe electrical installation.
28Language

Style Guide

The house rules of writing — citation, punctuation, capitalisation, and usage.

SeeThe Chicago Manual of Style — the editors' standard since 1906; APA and MLA rule their own fields.
29Law

Casebook

A teaching collection of real cases — judicial opinions or business studies — read to learn a field.

SeeThe law-school casebook — curated opinions, in the case method Christopher Langdell built at Harvard in the 1870s.
30History

Sourcebook

Gathers primary texts — the documents themselves — into one volume for study.

SeeSources of Chinese Tradition (Wm. Theodore de Bary, ed.) — foundational Chinese texts collected for readers.
31Law

Digest

Condenses and re-sorts a mass of material by subject, so the law on a point can be found fast.

SeeAmerican Law Reports and West's digests — cases summarised and arranged by legal topic.
32General

Who's Who

A directory of notable living people — potted biographies of the eminent, updated each year.

SeeWho's Who — the British directory of notable people, published annually since 1849; Marquis Who's Who in America.
33History

Prosopography

Collective biography — the assembled lives of a whole group, so patterns across them emerge.

SeeThe Prosopography of the Later Roman Empire (Jones, Martindale & Morris, 3 vols, 1971–92) — everyone attested AD 260–641.
34Religion

Calendar

Fixes the cycle of dates and observances — religious feasts, civic dates, celestial events.

SeeThe liturgical calendar of feasts and fasts; in astronomy, the celestial calendar of The Nautical Almanac.

Section IV

Reference by Domain

Beyond the general families, most disciplines have evolved their own house set of reference tools. The same underlying types recur — a dictionary, an index, a catalogue — but tuned to the field's particular texts and questions. Here is how six domains equip their shelves, each entry paired with a standard work.

Language

  • Dictionary — meanings and usage; e.g. Merriam-Webster's Collegiate.
  • Thesaurus — words grouped by idea; Roget's Thesaurus.
  • Lexicon — a specialised-language dictionary; Liddell & Scott (Greek), Lane (Arabic).
  • Concordance — every occurrence of a word in a text; Cruden's Concordance.
  • Glossary — terms defined for one subject or book.
  • Phrasebook — ready-made expressions for travellers; the Berlitz phrasebooks.
  • Etymological dictionary — word origins traced back through languages; Klein's Comprehensive Etymological Dictionary.
  • Pronouncing dictionary — how words are said; Daniel Jones's English Pronouncing Dictionary (Cambridge).

History

  • Chronology — events fixed in sequence; The Timetables of History.
  • Historical atlas — maps of the past as it changed; The Times Atlas of World History.
  • Genealogical register — family lines and descent; Burke's Peerage.
  • Biographical dictionary — lives of the notable dead; the Oxford Dictionary of National Biography.
  • Historical gazetteer — places as they were named and known; Smith's Dictionary of Greek and Roman Geography.

Science

  • CRC Handbook (constants) — physical constants and data; the CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics.
  • Pharmacopoeia — official drug standards; the United States Pharmacopeia (USP).
  • Taxonomic catalog — the named species of life; the Catalogue of Life.
  • Star catalog — positions and magnitudes of stars; the Hipparcos Catalogue.
  • Ephemeris — predicted positions of celestial bodies; The Astronomical Almanac.

Religion

  • Concordance — every word of scripture, located; المعجم المفهرس of ʿAbd al-Bāqī for the Qurʾān.
  • Bible / Qurʾān dictionary — terms and names explained; al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī's Mufradāt alfāẓ al-Qurʾān.
  • Commentary (tafsir, exegesis) — verse-by-verse interpretation; al-Ṭabarī's Jāmiʿ al-bayān.
  • Hadith index — reports located across the collections; Wensinck's al-Muʿjam al-mufahras li-alfāẓ al-ḥadīth.
  • Canon law collections — a tradition's codified law; the Catholic Code of Canon Law (1983).
  • Liturgical calendar — the ordered cycle of feasts and fasts across the year.

Philosophy

  • Companion — a guided map of the field; the Cambridge Companion series.
  • Encyclopedia of Philosophy — survey articles on problems and figures; the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.
  • Dictionary of Philosophy — concise definitions of terms; Blackburn's Oxford Dictionary of Philosophy.
  • Anthology / Reader — key texts gathered for study.
  • Sourcebook — primary passages in translation; A Source Book in Indian Philosophy (Radhakrishnan & Moore).

Business & Economics

  • Statistical yearbook — annual national and global figures; the UN Statistical Yearbook.
  • Financial handbook — the working formulas and conventions of finance.
  • Market directory — who supplies and buys within a sector.
  • Industry report — the state and outlook of a sector; IBISWorld reports.
  • Economic indicators reference — the headline gauges of an economy; the OECD Main Economic Indicators.
Section V

Building a Reference Shelf

If you wanted to assemble one well-rounded reference shelf — the fifteen works that between them answer most everyday factual questions — it would look something like this. Each entry covers a different kind of question, which is exactly why libraries have kept them close at hand for centuries.

  1. Dictionary — what a word means.
  2. Encyclopedia — what a thing is.
  3. Atlas — where it is, in maps.
  4. Almanac — this year's facts and figures.
  5. Thesaurus — the better word for it.
  6. Handbook — a field's working facts and formulas.
  7. Style Guide — how to write and cite it.
  8. Field Guide — how to identify what you see.
  9. Timeline / Chronology — when it happened.
  10. Biographical Dictionary — who the person was.
  11. Gazetteer — what a place is like, in words.
  12. Statistical Yearbook — the numbers behind the world.
  13. Sourcebook — the primary documents themselves.
  14. Concordance — (if studying religious or literary texts) where a word occurs.
  15. Companion — (for a specialised subject) how the field is organised.
Section VI

The Digital Turn

Reference works were the first genre of book to move online in earnest, and for a simple reason: lookup is exactly what hyperlinks and search boxes do best. A book built for retrieval loses little when it leaves paper — and gains speed, cross-linking, and constant revision.

The landmarks are recent. Encyclopædia Britannica stopped printing in 2012 after nearly two and a half centuries; the default general encyclopedia is now Wikipedia, launched in 2001. The Oxford English Dictionary has been online since 2000, and its third edition is being compiled not as a new fixed set of volumes but as a continuous revision. The Statistical Abstract of the United States survived the end of its print run as a live ProQuest database. Corpora like COCA simply keep growing; the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy is peer-reviewed and updated entry by entry, never “finished.”

The forms dissolve into software; the functions remain exactly what they were.

Look closely and the old categories are still everywhere, only hidden inside the tools we use without naming. A search box is a concordance and an index. A mapping app is an atlas welded to a gazetteer. A spell-checker carries a dictionary; autocomplete runs on a corpus; a citation manager is a portable style guide. The shelf has been folded into the screen, but the questions have not changed — and neither has the discipline of knowing which kind of answer you are looking for.

That, in the end, is what a field guide to reference works is for. Not to memorise thirty-four labels, but to recognise the shape of your question quickly enough to reach for the right tool — on the shelf or in the search bar — and get back the fact you came for.
