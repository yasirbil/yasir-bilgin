Jewish Intellectual and Artistic Achievement

Why Jewish communities have been so prominent in science, scholarship and the arts — a fact-checked look at culture, history and economics, and at the claims that don’t hold up.

YB
Yasir Bilgin Expanded from the Nov 2, 2023 Medium essay · Sept 2026
21 min read
The short answer

No single factor explains it, and the best-supported explanations are historical and institutional, not biological. A religious norm of near-universal male literacy, beginning in the first centuries CE, steered Jewish communities toward urban, literacy-rewarding work. Centuries as a minority built dense networks and a marginal vantage point. And when emancipation came, it opened universities, laboratories and stages almost all at once. The pattern is also narrower than “Jews” suggests — concentrated in particular places and eras — and it is easily turned into a stereotype, so this essay handles it with care.

Numbers in brackets, like [1], point to the sources at the end. Gold Fact-check notes flag places where a popular claim is more nuanced than it sounds.

Section I

The Question, Carefully Framed

Before asking why, it helps to be precise about what. The most-cited measure is the Nobel Prize. By one count, at least 220 of the 965 laureates between 1901 and 2025 were Jews or had at least one Jewish parent — about 22% — in a world where Jews make up roughly 0.2% of the population[3][5]. The share varies by field: about 40% in economics, 26% in physiology or medicine, 24% in physics, 19% in chemistry, 14% in literature and 8% in peace[3].

≈22%of Nobel laureates, 1901–2025, are Jewish or have a Jewish parent[3]
≈0.2%of the world’s population is Jewish (nearly 15 million in 2020)[5]
6 in 10U.S. Jewish adults hold a college degree, versus about 3 in 10 of all U.S. adults[4]

The U.S. picture is similar. In Pew’s 2020 survey, nearly six in ten Jewish adults were college graduates, including 28% with a postgraduate degree, versus about three in ten U.S. adults overall and 11% with a postgraduate degree. And 23% of Jewish Americans reported family incomes of $200,000 or more, compared with 4% of U.S. adults[4].

These are real patterns. But four cautions apply to every explanation that follows:

  • The definition is broad. The Nobel count includes people with a single Jewish parent, which is wider than religious or communal definitions[3].
  • The pattern is concentrated. The celebrated examples come mainly from Jewish communities in modern Europe and North America. Jewish achievement has varied enormously by place and century, so it is not a timeless trait.
  • Extremes are not averages. Prizes measure the far tail. Most Jews across history were ordinary artisans, traders and laborers, many of them poor.
  • A pattern needs a cause, not a label. An over-representation of this kind is a puzzle for historians and social scientists, not evidence of a fixed group characteristic.
Section II

Culture: Education, Text and Debate

2.1Education as a religious duty

Education holds a central place in Jewish tradition, and it is written into the classical texts. The Mishnah tractate Pirkei Avot (5:21) lays out a life curriculum: “At five years the age is reached for studying Mikra, at ten for studying the Mishnah, at thirteen for fulfilling the mitzvoth, at fifteen for studying Talmud”[10]. The Talmud also credits a first-century sage, Joshua ben Gamla, with instituting schools in every town and making education compulsory from around age six or seven[10].

Two qualifications matter. First, the norm applied chiefly to boys and men; women’s access to formal study was generally limited until the modern era. Second, historians debate how far a fully organized school system really existed so early — one prominent critic doubts that rabbis could have created such a “perfected schooling system”[2]. The ideal of universal literacy is well attested; the exact machinery is less certain.

2.2Chavruta: learning by argument

The traditional study partnership, chavruta (from an Aramaic word meaning “fellowship”), typically pairs two to five students to read, discuss and debate a shared text rather than listen to a lecturer[9]. It has old roots: one saying urges, “Make for yourself a Rav and acquire for yourself a chaver,” and another warns that scholars who sit alone to study “become stupid.” The Talmud’s portrait of Rabbi Yochanan and his study partner captures the method: “whenever I would say something, he would pose 24 difficulties and I would give him 24 solutions”[9].

The plausible payoff is training in analysis, articulate speech, respect for opposing views and sustained focus. The evidence, however, is thin: early research suggests chavruta-style training may help students in law school, but causality is unclear[9]. It is a credible mechanism, not a proven one.

2.3Pilpul — a more complicated story

The original essay cites pilpul as a method that nurtures critical thinking. The word does mean “pepper,” a term for sharp textual analysis, and Maimonides ranked the analytical derivation of one concept from another as an essential kind of study[8]. But the history of the term is contested.

Fact-check — pilpul

Within Jewish tradition itself, pilpul became a term of criticism. Critics charged that some practitioners were more interested in impressing an audience with the ingenuity of an argument than in finding the truth, and mockingly renamed it bilbul (“confusion”). The Maharal of Prague reportedly suggested that carpentry or another trade would be more worthwhile than such pilpul[8]. So it is safer to credit the broader culture of close reading and debate — chavruta and Talmudic argument — than pilpul specifically.

2.4Languages

Jewish communities were often multilingual: Hebrew and Aramaic for sacred texts, a Jewish vernacular such as Yiddish, Ladino or Judeo-Arabic for home and community, and the language of the host society for trade and law. Maimonides is an example — he wrote most of his works in Judeo-Arabic and the Mishneh Torah in Hebrew[18].

Fact-check — multilingualism and “cognitive flexibility”

The original essay suggests multilingualism “may have contributed to cognitive flexibility.” That link is contested. Reviews of the “bilingual advantage” in executive functioning have found inconsistent results, with concerns about small samples and publication bias; a 2018 meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin reported no compelling evidence of an advantage in healthy adults[11]. The stronger, more practical argument is that several languages made a community valuable as translators, brokers and intermediaries — a point that returns in Section VII.

Section III

History: How a Literate People Became an Urban One

The single most influential recent account comes from economists Maristella Botticini and Zvi Eckstein in The Chosen Few (2012)[1]. Their argument, in outline[2]:

  1. After the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, Jewish religious leaders made Torah education for male children a communal norm, producing unusual literacy compared with surrounding populations.
  2. In agrarian societies, that literacy carried a cost with little immediate payoff — children studying were children not farming — and, they argue, many Jews chose to leave the community.
  3. With the rise of Islam and the growth of cities in the 8th–9th centuries, literacy suddenly paid. Jews moved into crafts, commerce and finance, where literacy, numeracy, networks and contract-enforcement institutions were an advantage.
  4. European rulers later invited Jewish settlers to exploit those skills, spreading Jewish communities across the continent.

The book’s reception was mixed. An economist praised it as a very convincing cliometric analysis, while historians objected to a single unified explanation for developments that were “protracted and complex,” and some disputed whether the claimed selection through conversion ever occurred at scale[2]. Treat it as the leading hypothesis, not a settled account.

3.1A timeline

  1. 70 CEDestruction of the Second Temple; rabbinic emphasis on Torah study and education intensifies (the Botticini–Eckstein starting point)[2].
  2. 8th–9th centuriesIslamic urban expansion; Jews increasingly specialize in crafts, commerce and finance[2].
  3. c. 1135–1204Maimonides: born in Córdoba, died in Fustat (Old Cairo); court physician in the Ayyubid circle and author of the Mishneh Torah and Guide for the Perplexed[18].
  4. 1492–1493Expulsion from Spain; Sephardic refugees allowed to settle in Ottoman cities under Sultan Bayezid II; a Hebrew press opens in Istanbul in 1493[17].
  5. 1791–1871Emancipation: France (1791), Britain (1858), Austria-Hungary (1867), Germany (1871)[14].
  6. 1887–1920sQuotas: the Russian Empire (1887), Hungary (1920), and informal caps at elite U.S. universities[15].
  7. 1933Nazi civil-service law dismisses Jewish and other scholars; a scientific exodus follows[16].

3.2Were Jews “forced” into intellectual professions?

The original essay says historical restrictions forced Jews into finance, medicine and law. Part of that is right, and part needs adjusting.

Fact-check — “restrictions forced them into professions”

Sequence matters. In the Botticini–Eckstein account, literacy came first, and the move into urban trades and finance in the Islamic world came before the heaviest restrictions in Christian Europe[2]. Later exclusions clearly shaped the picture: historians commonly note that barriers to guilds and landholding, together with the Church’s ban on Christian lending at interest, channeled many European Jews toward trade and lending. Law is largely anachronistic. Access to universities and to the legal profession as a group came mainly with emancipation in the 19th century[14]. Medicine is a partial case: Jewish physicians were prominent much earlier — Maimonides served as court physician in Egypt — but as individuals with private training, not through open university admission[18].

3.3Persecution, resilience and “natural selection”

The original essay suggests that intense persecution “could have resulted in natural selection of resilient communities.” It rightly calls this sensitive. Three problems are worth naming.

  • Survivorship bias. We study the communities and individuals that survived and flourished; expulsions and massacres destroyed many that did not. Looking only at survivors makes hardship look like a training program.
  • There is no biological evidence for the mechanism. Biological “selection” for achievement by persecution is speculative at best (see Section VI).
  • A cultural version is more defensible. The Botticini–Eckstein “selection” runs through religious choice — those who stayed in the community were those who kept investing in education — and even that mechanism is disputed[2].

Individual psychological resilience remains a plausible contributing factor, as the original notes, but it is hard to test at the level of a whole people, and persecution also imposed enormous costs — lives, wealth, institutions and scholarship lost. The 1933 dismissals show the other side of the ledger: the 15% of German physicists dismissed accounted for 64% of German physics citations, a reminder of how much a society loses when it drives talent away (the law targeted political opponents as well as people of Jewish ancestry)[16].

Section IV

Sociology: The Vantage Point of a Minority

4.1Marginality and the “outsider’s view”

The original essay says being a “perpetual minority” can offer unique perspectives. This is an old idea. In 1919 the American economist Thorstein Veblen argued in “The Intellectual Pre-eminence of Jews in Modern Europe” that Jewish scholars in modern Europe were partly outside both their own tradition and the majority culture’s inherited habits of thought, and that this in-between position encouraged skepticism and fresh questions[13]. It is a hypothesis about a position, not about a people: anyone placed on a cultural boundary might gain similar leverage.

But marginality was rarely a privilege. Gustav Mahler converted to Catholicism in February 1897, apparently as a pragmatic step to overcome the bar on a Jew directing the Vienna Court Opera — and still faced relentless hostility from the antisemitic press, including a campaign in 1907 to drive him out[20]. The outsider’s view came bundled with real costs.

4.2Jews as a “middleman minority”

Sociologists use the term middleman minority for a minority concentrated in trade, finance and services that fill gaps between elites and consumers without carrying much political power. The concept was developed in the 1960s by Hubert Blalock and Edna Bonacich[12]. Listed examples include Ottoman Greeks and Armenians, Parsis and Gujaratis in India, Overseas Chinese in Southeast Asia, Indians in East Africa, Lebanese in West Africa and Jewish communities in Europe and the Americas[12].

The comparison matters for two reasons. It suggests that structure — a minority position, tight networks, literacy and commerce — explains part of the pattern, since similar profiles appear in unrelated groups. And it flags a danger: middleman minorities tend to face discrimination despite their success and can be scapegoated in periods of economic or political instability, often through stereotypes of “greed or clannishness”[12].

4.3Community institutions and networks

The original essay points to yeshivot, synagogues, charitable funds (tzedakah) and transnational links between communities. These institutions lowered the cost of learning — a scholarship-like support for study — and moved ideas, correspondence and trade across borders. The claim is widely made and historically plausible, but hard to measure precisely; it is best read as a supporting factor that enables the literacy norm rather than an independent cause.

Section V

Economics: A Loop, and What Exclusion Reveals

5.1Education and income feed each other

The original essay says economic success can be turned into educational opportunity, creating a cycle that reinforces achievement across generations. The mechanism is straightforward: schooling raises earnings, and earnings pay for the next generation’s schooling, tutors, books and connections. The U.S. numbers in Section I fit that pattern: nearly six in ten Jewish adults are college graduates and 23% report family incomes of $200,000 or more, against about three in ten and 4% for U.S. adults overall[4].

Limits — what a loop can and cannot explain

A feedback loop maintains an advantage; it does not say where the advantage began. It also runs in every direction: any community that gains access to schooling can enter the same cycle. And the Pew figures describe U.S. Jews in 2020, not Jews everywhere or across history. Averages also hide variation: a group can have a high average and still contain many households that are struggling.

5.2What the quotas reveal

One of the clearest signs of Jewish academic success is a grim one: governments and universities capped it. Quotas appear in several countries, and each shows both a pattern and a hostility to it[15].

Fact-check — “success in spite of restrictions”

It is tempting to read this history as “restriction produced achievement.” The record shows the opposite pattern: where access opened, Jewish enrollment rose fast; where it was capped, the caps limited it. Exclusion was a cost imposed on talent, not a training program.

5.3The cost of driving talent away

The 1933 dismissals show the cost from the other side. On April 7, 1933, Germany’s Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service ordered the removal of officials with Jewish ancestry or opposition to the Nazi Party. Among the émigrés were Albert Einstein, Hans Krebs and Fritz Haber; on a 1936 list of 129 displaced physicists, Nobel laureates included Einstein, James Franck and Erwin Schrödinger, with future laureates such as Hans Bethe, Max Born and Otto Stern. About 30 physicists went to the United States and more than 37 to the United Kingdom[16]. The list covers people displaced for political as well as ancestral reasons — Schrödinger, for instance, was not Jewish and left in protest. But the direction of travel is clear: institutions that had helped produce great science lost it, and institutions that welcomed the refugees gained it.

Section VI

The Biology Question, Fact-Checked

The original essay notes that some researchers suggest genetic factors may influence high average IQ scores among Ashkenazi Jews, and that such theories face “considerable skepticism and ethical scrutiny.” That is a fair summary. This section says more about what the claim is and why the skepticism is warranted.

6.1What was actually claimed

The best-known version is a 2006 paper by Gregory Cochran, Jason Hardy and Henry Harpending in the Journal of Biosocial Science. It proposed that, in medieval Europe, Ashkenazi Jews were under selective pressure because they were confined to occupations such as money-lending that rewarded verbal and mathematical ability. It also suggested that inherited diseases common in the community, such as Tay–Sachs and Gaucher’s, were by-products of that selection. The paper cites an average IQ “in a range of 108–115 under some studies”[6].

6.2Why it remains contested

  • The genetic evidence is thin. Geneticist David Goldstein called the hypothesis “speculative” and “circumstantial,” noting there is no empirical link between the disease alleles and cognition. Adam Rutherford reported that none of the disease genes highlighted showed associations with brain or cognitive ability in genetic databases[6].
  • A simpler explanation exists for the diseases. David Reich and others argue that genetic drift and founder effects explain their prevalence, and Bray and colleagues found that most disease loci in Ashkenazi Jews are not under strong positive selection[6].
  • The IQ figures themselves are disputed. Richard Nisbett’s group states that there is “little good evidence” about Ashkenazi IQ levels, and studies using polygenic scores have been criticized as methodologically flawed[6].
  • Social explanations fit better. Chad and Brym argue that “proximate sociological circumstances” explain variation over time better than genetic or cultural theories alone[6].

The broader scientific position, as summarized there, is that differences in average test performance between groups cannot be ascribed to genetics, and that environment, cultural emphasis on learning and access to opportunity are the more credible explanations[6].

6.3Three problems even if the numbers held

These are points of reasoning rather than findings, but they show why a biological account explains less than it seems to.

  1. The timing is wrong for a fixed trait. Gene pools do not change in a century, yet the celebrated pattern appears mainly after emancipation opened universities (Section III) — and the Nobel Prize itself only began in 1901. Whatever explains the pattern must be something that changed, such as access and institutions.
  2. The claim is about one branch. The hypothesis concerns Ashkenazi Jews specifically. It says nothing about Maimonides’ Sephardic world (Section VII) or other Jewish communities, so it cannot be a general explanation of “Jewish achievement.”
  3. Averages are not tails. Nobel-level work depends on training, mentors, laboratories and funding. A small difference in a distribution says little about who ends up in those places.
Bottom line

The biological hypothesis is speculative, has drawn substantial criticism from geneticists, and does not account for the timing or geography of the pattern. It is best treated as an unsettled claim, not a component of the explanation.

Section VII

Beyond Europe: Sepharad and the Ottoman World

Most discussion of Jewish achievement looks at Central Europe and North America. Widening the frame to the Islamic and Ottoman worlds shows that the pattern took other forms in other places.

7.1Maimonides: scholar, jurist, physician

Maimonides (born in Córdoba in 1135 or 1138, died in Fustat, Egypt, in 1204) held several careers at once. He served as court physician to Qadi al-Fadil, the chief secretary to Saladin, then to Saladin himself and to the Ayyubid dynasty after him. He wrote the Mishneh Torah (1170–1180), a comprehensive code of Jewish law, in Hebrew, and the philosophical Guide for the Perplexed (1185–1190) — most of his works were in Judeo-Arabic[18]. His career shows a Jewish scholar working at the heart of Arabic-language scientific and legal culture, centuries before the modern university.

7.2The Ottoman welcome, and what it was

After the 1492 Alhambra Decree, an influx of Jews reached Anatolia and the Ottoman Empire under Sultan Bayezid II (r. 1481–1512), and they were allowed to settle in the empire’s wealthier cities. One account puts Istanbul’s Jewish community at about 30,000 people with 44 synagogues. In 1493 David and Samuel ibn Nahmias set up a printing press — then a new technology that sped up the production of both religious texts and administrative documents[17].

The roles Jewish newcomers filled were specific. Physicians and diplomats worked for the state: Jewish doctors such as Moses Hamon and Ishak Pasha served sultans, and the diplomat Joseph Nasi was made Duke of Naxos[17]. Sociologists list Ottoman Jews, alongside Ottoman Greeks and Armenians, as examples of a middleman minority[12].

7.3What the contrast suggests

Two readings are reasonable, though neither is proven. First, the Ottoman case fits the Botticini–Eckstein step in which rulers invited Jewish communities for their skills[2]: a state that wanted physicians, printers and diplomats made room for them. Second, the achievements it produced — court medicine, statecraft, printing, commerce, religious scholarship — differ from the modern laboratory-and-university pattern behind most Nobel counts. Different institutions reward different kinds of excellence. The Nobel pattern is not a timeless Jewish trait; it is what happens when a highly literate community meets open research universities.

Section VIII

The Arts: Outsiders Who Built Institutions

The original essay’s title mentions artists as well as intellectuals, but its body says little about the arts. Two cases show how the same themes — outsider status, skills carried over from trade, and barriers — play out on stage and screen.

8.1Hollywood: building an industry from outside

In An Empire of Their Own (1988), the historian Neal Gabler argues that Jewish immigrant producers, excluded and alienated in America because of their religion, built Hollywood as an industry in which they could belong. He says the industry was open to them because many had experience in theater ownership, retail distribution and the garment trade, skills that carried over to the emerging studio system. Their motive, in his words, was to create “an empire of their own… an America where fathers were strong, families stable, people attractive, resilient, resourceful, and decent”[19].

Limits — one account, and a specific era

Gabler’s is an interpretive thesis, not a measured cause. It also describes the founders of the studio system in the early 20th century. It does not describe who owns or controls entertainment today, and treating it as if it did is how a historical observation turns into a conspiracy theory (see Section IX).

8.2Two kinds of outsider

Set beside Gustav Mahler’s story in Section IV, the contrast is useful. The studio founders built a new institution that no established gatekeeper controlled. Mahler had to enter an old one, and only did so by converting in 1897 to overcome the bar on a Jew directing the Vienna Court Opera — and he was still attacked in the press[20]. New fields with weak gatekeeping (early film) may be easier for outsiders to enter than established ones (court opera, ancient universities). That fits the wider point of this essay: what matters is often the structure of access, not a trait of the people who pass through it.

Section IX

Handling It With Care

The original essay ends by saying these factors are not unique to any one group and should not support generalizations or stereotypes. That deserves more than a sentence, because this topic has a long record of being turned against the people it praises.

9.1Positive stereotypes are not harmless

Scholars of antisemitism note that antisemitism and philosemitism (exaggerated admiration for Jews) overlap and share stereotypes. The scholar Bennet Kravitz argued that the admirable qualities Mark Twain attributed to Jews in “Concerning the Jews” could just as easily be used for hatred. Portraits of Jews as clever, wealthy and powerful can serve as rhetorical vehicles for conspiracy theories[7]. The middleman-minority literature makes the same point from sociology: minorities concentrated in commerce tend to be scapegoated in periods of economic or political instability, often through stereotypes of “greed or clannishness”[12]. Praise that treats a whole people as a single, unusually gifted or powerful bloc is one step from the trope that the same bloc is scheming.

9.2“The Jews” is not one group

The statistics in this essay blur an important fact: “Jewish” is defined differently in different data. The Nobel count includes anyone with at least one Jewish parent[3]. Pew’s U.S. estimate of 2.4% of adults includes 1.7% who identify with the Jewish religion and 0.6% who are Jewish by background but have no religion[4]. Worldwide, nearly 15 million people are Jewish, and about 85% of them live in just two countries, Israel and the United States[5]. Behind those totals are Ashkenazi, Sephardi, Mizrahi and other communities with different languages, histories and circumstances. Generalizations that begin “Jews are…” rarely survive contact with the data.

9.3Five habits for discussing it well

A working checklist
  1. Explain by conditions, not essence. Say “communities with a literacy norm and access to universities”, not “a talented people.”
  2. Name the time and place. Late-19th-century Vienna, 1930s Berlin and 1960s New York are different cases.
  3. Remember the tail. Prize winners are exceptional by definition and say little about a typical member of any group.
  4. Count the costs. Expulsions, quotas and genocide destroyed people, wealth, libraries and scholarship; “resilience” does not repay them.
  5. Distrust flattering explanations. An account that makes one group a special case is more likely to be a stereotype than a finding.
Section X

The Scorecard, and a Conclusion

10.1The best-supported story, in four steps

Putting the strongest pieces together gives a cautious sequence, not a single cause.

1. A norm From 70 CE

Torah study for boys becomes a communal duty, producing unusual literacy[2].

2. A niche 8th–15th centuries

Growing cities reward literacy, and Jews move into crafts, commerce and finance[2].

3. A network Across centuries

Community institutions and links between cities spread knowledge and trust — a supporting factor (see 4.3), hard to measure.

4. An opening 1791 onward

Emancipation admits Jews to universities and professions; a literate, urban community enters at once[14].

Exclusion and persecution run through every step, but as costs imposed on a community, not as the training program that a survivorship-biased story imagines.

10.3Conclusion

The original essay concludes that the success of Jewish intellectuals and artists reflects a complex interplay of cultural, historical, sociological and economic factors, and that these factors are not unique to any one group. The evidence supports that conclusion and sharpens it. The strongest explanations are institutional and historical: a long-standing norm of literacy, urban niches that rewarded it, dense networks, and the sudden opening of universities and stages. The weakest are the ones that attribute the pattern to something inherent in a people.

What is left is a lesson that applies well beyond this case. Communities that value learning, hold their institutions together under pressure and are given access to opportunity tend to flourish, and the same is true of many minorities in many places. The achievements of a few Jewish scientists and artists tell us what happens when literacy meets access. They tell us nothing about a special essence.

Not a trait of a people, but what happens when learning meets open doors.
